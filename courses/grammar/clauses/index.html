Complete sentences and run-ons

Open in StudyFox Free, no ads. Practice, track progress and quiz yourself in the app.

SAT Grammar Rules · Lesson 1 of 9 · 5 min read

Almost every punctuation question comes down to one idea: the independent clause. An independent clause has a subject and a verb and could stand alone as a sentence.

Two independent clauses can be joined only by: a period, a semicolon, a comma plus a FANBOYS conjunction (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so), or a colon (if the second explains the first).
IncorrectThe fossil was nearly complete, researchers were thrilled.
CorrectThe fossil was nearly complete. Researchers were thrilled.
CorrectThe fossil was nearly complete; researchers were thrilled.
CorrectThe fossil was nearly complete, so researchers were thrilled.

A comma alone between two independent clauses is a comma splice. It's the single most common wrong answer on the SAT.

Fragments

A fragment is missing a main verb or depends on another clause. "Although the fossil was nearly complete." is a fragment because although makes it dependent.

Tip Test each side of the punctuation mark: can it stand alone as a sentence? That tells you which punctuation is allowed.

Check your understanding

Choose the correct option: "The committee reviewed every proposal ______ none met the funding criteria."

  1. proposal,
  2. proposal
  3. proposal;
  4. proposal, and,
Show answer

Answer: C. Both sides are independent clauses, so a semicolon works. A comma alone is a splice, no punctuation is a run-on, and "and," puts a comma in the wrong place.
