Unit 5: Political Participation

Open in StudyFox Free, no ads. Practice, track progress and quiz yourself in the app.

AP U.S. Government and Politics · Lesson 6 of 6 · 8 min read

  • Voting: amendments expanded it (15th, 19th, 24th banned poll taxes, 26th set the age at 18). Turnout rises with age, education, and income. Models: rational choice, retrospective, prospective, and party-line voting.
  • Political parties recruit candidates, mobilize voters, and organize government. The U.S. has two major parties because of winner-take-all, single-member districts. Third parties struggle but can influence the major parties' platforms.
  • Interest groups lobby, file amicus briefs, and fund campaigns through PACs. The free-rider problem makes some groups hard to organize.
  • Elections: primaries and caucuses, national conventions, and the Electoral College (270 votes to win). Incumbents have big advantages.
  • Campaign finance: Citizens United v. FEC (2010) ruled that limiting independent political spending by corporations and unions violates free speech, leading to super PACs.
  • Media: the shift to online and partisan sources, horse-race coverage, and concerns about bias and misinformation. Linkage institutions (parties, interest groups, elections, media) connect people to government.

Why does the U.S. tend to have only two major parties?

  1. The Constitution limits it to two
  2. Winner-take-all, single-member districts make it hard for third parties to win seats
  3. Third parties are illegal
  4. The Supreme Court ruled it so
Show answer

Answer: B. When only the top vote-getter wins, voters avoid "wasting" votes on small parties, so two parties dominate (Duverger's law).
